Every office has one: the cupboard of dead laptops, tangled chargers, old UPS batteries and monitors nobody wants to throw away. International E-Waste Day, held every year on **14 October**, is a good moment to open that cupboard, because in India what happens next is no longer optional.

The **E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022**, which took effect on 1 April 2023, rebuilt the country's e-waste system around **Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)**. Here is what that means in practice.

## Who the rules apply to

The rules name several roles, and most businesses fit at least one:

- **Producers**: anyone who makes, imports or sells electrical and electronic equipment under their own brand, including online sellers.
- **Manufacturers and refurbishers**: units that make or repair equipment and generate e-waste while doing it.
- **Recyclers**: authorised facilities that recover materials from e-waste.
- **Bulk consumers**: large organisations that use electronics in volume: companies, government offices, hospitals, universities and similar institutions.

Producers, manufacturers, refurbishers and recyclers must register on the **Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) EPR portal**. Unregistered entities cannot legally trade in e-waste.

## How EPR works now

Under the old system, producers ran or paid for their own collection channels. The 2022 rules replaced that with a **certificate market**:

1. Registered recyclers process e-waste and generate **EPR certificates** on the portal for the material they recycle.
2. Producers have an annual recycling target based on the equipment they placed on the market in earlier years. Those targets rise over time.
3. Producers meet the target by buying certificates from registered recyclers. Refurbishers can generate certificates that defer a producer's liability.
4. Missing a target attracts **environmental compensation**, a financial penalty levied by CPCB.

The result is that recycling capacity, and the paperwork that proves it, now carries a price. That makes accurate, traceable data on where e-waste ends up valuable.

## What bulk consumers must do

If your organisation is a bulk consumer, your main duty is simple to state and easy to get wrong: **hand over e-waste only to a registered producer, refurbisher or recycler**, and keep a record of it.

A workable internal process looks like this:

- **Inventory.** Tag IT and electrical assets so you know what you own and when it is due for retirement.
- **Consolidate.** Collect end-of-life items at one point instead of letting them drift between departments.
- **Verify the recipient.** Check the recycler's or refurbisher's registration on the CPCB portal before handover.
- **Document.** Keep the handover receipts and quantities. They are your evidence of compliance, and they double as data for your ESG reporting.
- **Wipe first.** Data destruction is your responsibility, not the recycler's. Certify that drives are wiped or destroyed.

## Why this matters beyond compliance

E-waste contains valuable materials such as copper, aluminium, gold and rare earths alongside hazardous ones like lead, mercury and brominated flame retardants. When it goes to informal dismantlers, the valuable parts are recovered crudely and the hazardous parts end up in soil, water and workers' lungs.

Routing e-waste through registered channels is therefore one of the most direct, measurable circular-economy actions an organisation can take. And because each handover is recorded, it is also one of the easiest to report.
