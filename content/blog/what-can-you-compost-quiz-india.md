You want to compost, but every time you hold a peel or a leftover chapati, you wonder if it belongs in the bin. One wrong item can make the pile smell, attract flies or stall altogether. Here is a quick quiz on **what can you compost**, with clear answers and a simple method to start before World Soil Day on 5 December.

## Key takeaways

- Most **raw fruit and vegetable waste**, tea leaves, coffee grounds, eggshells and dry leaves can go into home compost.
- **Meat, fish, dairy, oily cooked food, pet waste and diseased plants** are best kept out of a home bin.
- A good pile needs a balance of **wet "greens"** and **dry "browns"**.
- Compost is ready when it looks dark and crumbly and smells like earth.
- World Soil Day is on 5 December, a good moment to start.

## Why does composting matter for soil?

Soil is alive. It holds microbes, fungi and tiny organisms that feed plants and hold water. Compost returns organic matter and nutrients to that soil, instead of sending kitchen scraps to a landfill where they decompose without air and produce methane.

World Soil Day on **5 December** is a useful reminder that healthy soil begins with what we give back to it. For most households, wet waste is the biggest part of what they throw out each day, so it is also the easiest place to begin.

## What can you compost? Take the quiz

Answer each one in your head first, then read the answer.

**1. Banana peels, vegetable scraps and fruit rinds?**
Yes. These are the classic **greens**. Chop them small so they break down faster.

**2. Onion and garlic peels, and citrus peels?**
Yes, in moderation. Large amounts of citrus and onion can slow the pile down. Mix them with dry leaves and add them in small batches.

**3. Used tea leaves and coffee grounds?**
Yes. Remove any plastic-based tea bags first, since many contain plastic.

**4. Eggshells?**
Yes. Crush them well. They break down slowly but add useful minerals.

**5. Cooked rice, dal, rotis and leftovers?**
Better avoided in a basic home bin. Cooked and oily food can smell and attract rats, flies and cockroaches. Small amounts of plain cooked grain are manageable in a well-run, closed system.

**6. Meat, fish, bones and dairy?**
No, not in a simple home setup. They rot with a strong smell and draw pests.

**7. Dry leaves, paper scraps, cardboard and coconut husk?**
Yes. These are your **browns**. Tear or shred them before adding.

**8. Flowers and puja offerings?**
Yes, if they are natural. Remove plastic, metal wire, threads and any foil.

**9. Pet waste, diapers, sanitary products?**
No. They carry pathogens and are not suitable for compost used on plants.

**10. Glossy paper, plastic, treated wood, stickers on fruit?**
No. Peel off fruit stickers and keep anything with coatings or plastic out.

## Compost do's and don'ts at a glance

| Add freely | Add in small amounts | Keep out |
|---|---|---|
| Fruit and vegetable scraps | Citrus and onion peels | Meat, fish, bones |
| Tea leaves, coffee grounds | Plain cooked grains | Dairy and oily food |
| Crushed eggshells | Bread and cut flowers (shredded) | Pet waste, diapers |
| Dry leaves, shredded paper | Weeds without seeds | Plastic, glossy paper, stickers |
| Coconut husk, natural flowers | | Diseased plants |

## How do you start composting at home?

You do not need a garden. A balcony, terrace or even a large pot with a lid is enough to begin.

1. **Pick a container.** Use a terracotta pot, a compost bin or a bucket with holes for air and drainage.
2. **Start with a dry layer.** Add a few centimetres of dry leaves, coco peat or shredded paper at the bottom.
3. **Add kitchen waste.** Chop scraps small and spread them in a thin layer.
4. **Cover with browns.** Top each batch of wet waste with dry material. A rough guide is more dry matter than you think you need.
5. **Keep it moist, not soggy.** It should feel like a wrung-out sponge.
6. **Turn it regularly.** Mix every few days so air reaches the microbes.
7. **Be patient.** Depending on your setup and weather, it can take a couple of months to become dark, crumbly compost.

## Why does my compost smell, and how do I fix it?

Most problems come from an imbalance.

### The pile smells sour or rotten
It is usually too wet or has too many greens. Add dry leaves or shredded paper, and turn it to bring in air.

### Flies or fruit flies appear
Bury fresh scraps under a layer of browns and keep the lid on. Avoid adding cooked or oily food.

### The pile looks dry and nothing happens
Sprinkle some water and add more greens, such as vegetable scraps.

### Ants or rodents show up
Check for cooked food, dairy or meat. Remove them and keep the bin closed.

## What if I cannot compost at home?

Not every home has space or time, and that is fine. The key step is still **segregating wet waste at source**, so it can be processed separately instead of mixing with dry recyclables. Many housing societies use shared composters, and some cities have collection systems for wet waste.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I compost onion peels and citrus peels?
Yes, but in small amounts. Both break down more slowly and can make the pile acidic or sharp-smelling if you add too much. Mix them with dry leaves and add them in small batches.

### Can I put cooked food in my compost bin?
It is best to avoid it in a basic home bin. Cooked, oily or salty food attracts pests and creates odour. If you must add a little plain cooked grain, bury it under dry material.

### Can I compost paper and cardboard?
Plain paper, newspaper and cardboard can be shredded and used as browns. Avoid glossy, coated or heavily printed paper, and remove tape and plastic.

### How long does home compost take to be ready?
It depends on the size of the pile, the mix of materials, moisture and weather. Compost is ready when it looks dark and crumbly and smells earthy, with no recognisable food scraps left.

### Is composting only for people with gardens?
No. A pot, bucket or balcony bin works well, and you can use the finished compost for houseplants. If you have no use for it, a neighbour, community garden or society garden will usually welcome it.
